# 100 Great Copywriting Ideas (Andy Maslen) — Data Sampler Summary

**Are you looking for a way to write copy that actually converts instead of just sounding clever?** Have you ever stared at a blank page, knowing you need to sell but hating the feeling of becoming "salesy"? **Do you suspect there's a repeatable formula behind great copy, but every book you've picked up feels too theoretical to use?** Hey, my name is Will, and if you answered "yes" to any of these questions, this article is for you.

Most copywriting advice falls into two useless camps: the "be creative" camp that offers zero structure, and the "follow this exact template" camp that makes your writing sound like a robot. What's missing is the middle ground — the psychological machinery underneath the words. Without that, you're just guessing, and guessing doesn't pay the bills.

You could keep hunting through blog posts collecting headline formulas, or you could buy another expensive course that teaches you how to write like *that specific guru*. But these approaches give you tactics without understanding. You end up with a toolbox full of tools you don't know when to use.

The best solution I've found is Andy Maslen's deceptively simple approach in **100 Great Copywriting Ideas**. This isn't a book of cheesy templates — it's a collection of psychological patterns distilled from decades of direct-response experience. In this summary, I've extracted the four most powerful patterns from Maslen's book — the ones that turn skeptical readers into nodding buyers before they even realise what's happening.

These patterns are remarkably effective in the right context. But they're not universal — and later, I'll show you exactly where they fail so you don't waste them on the wrong audience.

---

## The One Thing I'd Steal First

If you close this tab and remember nothing else, remember this: **write to one person, not a demographic.**

Maslen's litmus test for tone: imagine your ideal customer sitting across from you at a pub. Would you say to them, "Our synergistic platform leverages cutting-edge AI to optimise your workflow"? Or would you say, "You know that hour you waste every morning sorting emails? This sorts them for you"? The second version wins every time because it sounds like a human talking to a human.

**Try this today:** Grab your last piece of copy. Read it aloud to an imaginary friend. Cut every sentence that sounds like it came from a press release instead of that conversation. Count your cuts. That number is how much more human your next draft will be.

---

## Core Insight

Maslen's central argument, as I see it:

> Effective copywriting is not about showcasing creativity or impressing readers with clever language — it is about understanding human psychology so deeply that you can make one person, reading alone, feel understood enough to act.

This single sentence reframes everything. Most of us were taught to write to impress — our English teachers, our creative directors, our peers. Maslen argues that none of those people are paying you. The only person who matters is the one reading your words in solitude, asking a silent question: *"Does this person get me?"* If the answer is no, they're gone. If it's yes, they'll follow you anywhere.

## The Four Patterns That Change Everything

### 1. Find the Pain Point, Then Be the Cure

**The Scenario:** Maslen teaches that every purchase is an emotional rescue disguised as a rational transaction. He describes opening a sales letter for a financial newsletter not by praising the newsletter, but by conjuring the reader's 3 a.m. demon: the gnawing fear that they're making investment decisions based on half-information, that one bad call could unravel years of savings. Only after amplifying that specific, visceral anxiety does he introduce the product as the antidote.

**The Pattern:** Open with the wound, not the bandage. Describe their 3 a.m. thought before you describe your solution. **Don't sell the life raft — first make them feel the cold water.**

**Why This Matters:** Readers have built-in defenses against sales pitches, but they have no immunity against feeling understood. Pain-based copy cuts through scepticism because it validates the reader's reality before asking for anything.

---

### 2. Write to One Person in a Bar

**The Scenario:** Corporate voice creates distance; conversational voice creates connection. **Replace "we are pleased to announce" with "here's the thing."** Replace "utilise" with "use." Replace demographic abstractions ("our target segment") with a single, specific person you can picture — their fears, their late-night worries, the exact words they'd use to describe their frustration.

**The Pattern:** Before you write, answer three questions about your ideal reader: What do they want? What keeps them up at night? How do they talk about their problem when they're with friends? **Write your first draft as a direct message to that one person.**

**Why This Matters:** People don't share, trust, or buy from institutions — they do those things with other people. The moment your copy feels like it was written by a committee, the reader's emotional engagement evaporates.

---

### 3. Get Them Nodding

**The Scenario:** Maslen's technique for opening direct mail: start with three questions the reader cannot help but answer "yes" to. "Do you ever feel like you're working harder but getting less done? Wish you had just two extra hours in your day? Tired of productivity advice that sounds good but never sticks?" By the time they reach your offer, they've already agreed with you three times — creating psychological momentum toward a fourth "yes."

**The Pattern:** Agreement is a habit. Every time a reader mentally says "yes" to your copy, they lower their defence threshold. **Structure your opening as a series of undeniable observations about the reader's own experience,** so that by the time you present your solution, it feels like their idea — not yours.

**Why This Matters:** The biggest barrier in copywriting is not informing — it's overcoming inertia. A reader who has already agreed with you is psychologically predisposed to continue agreeing, because humans value internal consistency above almost everything else.

---

### 4. Steal the Idea, Not the Execution

**The Scenario:** Maslen tells the story of seeing a brilliant perfume ad: a single image of a woman in a red dress walking through a crowd in black and white. The idea was "stand out." He stole that core concept — not the image, not the layout, not the tagline — and adapted it for a B2B software client: "While your competitors blend into the background, our clients get noticed." Same psychological trigger, completely different execution.

**The Pattern:** Creative originality is overrated; strategic adaptation is underrated. The best copywriters are magpies — they collect the underlying *ideas* that make campaigns work (isolation, contrast, transformation, belonging) and re-skin them for new contexts. **Copy the structure, copy the psychology, never copy the words.**

**Why This Matters:** Waiting for divine inspiration produces inconsistency. Building a swipe file of *concepts* and learning to recognise the psychological machinery beneath great copy turns writing into a craft you can execute on demand — even on days when you don't feel creative.

## The Maslen Formula

After distilling all 100 ideas, a clear equation emerges:

> **Effective Copy = (Specific Pain × Conversational Voice × Sequential Yeses) − Corporate Jargon**

Here's what this looks like in practice: start by naming a pain so specific it stings (not "boost your productivity" but "stop losing two hours a day to emails you'll never read"). Deliver that message in a voice so conversational it sounds like a friend texting. Build agreement step by step — never ask for the sale until they've nodded at least three times. And ruthlessly delete any phrase that sounds like it came from a boardroom.

## Critical Questions About This Framework

Before you implement these patterns everywhere, you need to know where they actually work — and where they fail. Every persuasion technique has a context where it thrives and a context where it backfires.

**Q: Does pain-based opening work for readers who don't feel the pain yet?**

Not really. Maslen's framework assumes problem-awareness. You name their pain, they recognise it, they feel relief when you offer a solution.

But what if they don't know they have a problem? A small business owner who's never been hacked doesn't feel cybersecurity anxiety at 3 a.m. A doctor's office comfortable with their paper records isn't worried about patient management software. They're not in pain — they're comfortable. Opening with their fear doesn't work. You need education first, then the pain-based approach.

**Works brilliantly for:** Direct-response campaigns, SaaS onboarding, services with clear immediate pain points.
**Struggles with:** Awareness-stage selling, enterprise sales, brand-building content.

---

**Q: Does "write to one person in a bar" work in all industries?**

No. Conversational tone is powerful — but it's not universal.

Try this voice in a legal document. Suddenly you sound inexperienced, and your client second-guesses your expertise. In regulated financial advice, casual tone creates liability. In luxury goods, formality and mystique are part of the perceived value. Even in B2B, CTOs and procurement teams have formal communication norms — violate them and you signal you don't understand their world.

**Works brilliantly for:** Ecommerce, SaaS marketing, direct-response, services with informal buyer psychology.
**Struggles with:** Luxury goods, regulated industries, enterprise sales to executives, professional services.

---

**Q: Can you really reduce persuasion to a formula?**

The formula is catchy, but it suggests mathematical precision where none exists.

What happens when pain = 0? Does multiplying by voice help? No — the whole equation fails. What if voice is wrong for your context? Does sequential agreement compensate? No — it tanks trust. These elements interact in complex, nonlinear ways that no simple math captures. The formula is useful as a memory aid, not as a literal tool. Treat it as a checklist of elements to consider, not an equation to calculate.

---

**Q: Are there downsides to these patterns?**

Every persuasion technique has trade-offs:

- **Pain-based copy** can backfire if readers don't feel the pain or resent emotional manipulation. Overuse makes you sound like a fearmonger.
- **Conversational voice** can damage authority in contexts where expertise matters. Sometimes readers *want* formality — it signals seriousness.
- **Agreement momentum** can reveal manipulation to skeptical buyers, tanking trust immediately. Sophisticated audiences recognise the pattern and resent it.
- **Stealing ideas** can produce derivative work that sounds like every other Maslen-trained copywriter. When everyone adapts the same patterns, no one stands out.

None of this makes the patterns bad. It just means they have costs. Know them before you apply them.

---

**Q: What about competing frameworks?**

Maslen's book focuses on direct-response psychology. It's exceptional at what it covers — but it doesn't cover everything. Here's how it fits alongside other major approaches:

| Framework | Focus | When to Use Instead of Maslen |
|-----------|-------|-------------------------------|
| **Ogilvy on Advertising** | Brand-building, long-term positioning | When you're building a brand, not just converting a sale |
| **StoryBrand (Donald Miller)** | Positioning the customer as hero | When your product supports a transformation narrative |
| **Jobs to Be Done (Christensen)** | Understanding the functional problem being hired for | When emotional pain isn't the primary driver |
| **Positioning (Ries & Trout)** | Owning mental real estate in the buyer's mind | When you're entering a crowded market and need differentiation |

**The takeaway:** Maslen's patterns are a powerful tool — for a specific job. Choose your framework based on what you're actually selling, not what you read most recently.

## Conclusion

Maslen's 100 ideas distill to one discipline: **write for the one person reading alone, not the boardroom that approved the budget.** Start with their pain, speak like a human, earn agreement step by step, and measure everything by action taken.

The patterns in this book work exceptionally well for what they're designed for: direct-response copy where the reader is already problem-aware and ready to make a decision. They're the right tool for the right job — and when the job fits, they're devastatingly effective.

But they're one tool, not the whole toolbox.

Use them where they fit. Question them where they don't. And always ask the question that separates great copywriters from formula-followers: **Does my reader already know they have this problem?**

If the answer is yes, you now have a battle-tested playbook. If the answer is no, you now know what to reach for instead.

Now go write something that actually works for *your* reader. And measure whether it does.
