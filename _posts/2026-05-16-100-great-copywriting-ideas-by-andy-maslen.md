# 100 Great Copywriting Ideas (Andy Maslen) — Data Sampler Summary

**Are you looking for a way to write copy that actually converts instead of just sounding clever?** Have you ever stared at a blank page, knowing you need to sell but hating the feeling of becoming "salesy"? **Do you suspect there's a repeatable formula behind great copy, but every book you've picked up feels too theoretical to use?** Hey, my name is Will, and if you answered "yes" to any of these questions, this article is for you.

Most copywriting advice falls into two useless camps: the "be creative" camp that offers zero structure, and the "follow this exact template" camp that makes your writing sound like a robot. What's missing is the middle ground — the psychological machinery underneath the words. Without that, you're just guessing, and guessing doesn't pay the bills.

You could keep hunting through blog posts collecting headline formulas, or you could buy another expensive course that teaches you how to write like *that specific guru*. But these approaches give you tactics without understanding. You end up with a toolbox full of tools you don't know when to use.

The best solution I've found is Andy Maslen's deceptively simple approach in **100 Great Copywriting Ideas**. This isn't a book of cheesy templates — it's a collection of psychological patterns distilled from decades of direct-response experience. In this summary, I've extracted the four most powerful patterns from Maslen's book — the ones that turn skeptical readers into nodding buyers before they even realise what's happening.

---

## The One Thing I'd Steal First

If you close this tab and remember nothing else, remember this: **people don't buy products — they buy freedom from pain.**

Maslen describes opening a sales letter for a financial newsletter not by praising the newsletter, but by conjuring the reader's 3 a.m. demon: the gnawing fear that they're making investment decisions based on half-information, that one bad call could unravel years of savings. Only after amplifying that specific, visceral anxiety does he introduce the product as the antidote.

**Try this today:** Before you write another word of copy, ask yourself — what is my reader afraid of at 3 a.m.? Write that fear down verbatim. Now write your opening sentence as if you're answering that exact fear. Watch what happens to your conversion rate.

---

## Core Insight

> Effective copywriting is not about showcasing creativity or impressing readers with clever language — it is about understanding human psychology so deeply that you can make one person, reading alone, feel understood enough to act.

This single sentence reframes everything. Most of us were taught to write to impress — our English teachers, our creative directors, our peers. Maslen argues that none of those people are paying you. The only person who matters is the one reading your words in solitude, asking a silent question: *"Does this person get me?"* If the answer is no, they're gone. If it's yes, they'll follow you anywhere.

## The Four Patterns That Change Everything

### 1. Find the Pain Point, Then Be the Cure

**The Scenario:** Maslen teaches that every purchase is an emotional rescue disguised as a rational transaction. Before you write a single benefit, identify the specific fear, frustration, or insecurity your reader experiences in their weakest moment — then position your product as the exact relief they were searching for.

**The Pattern:** Open with the wound, not the bandage. Describe their 3 a.m. thought before you describe your solution. **Don't sell the life raft — first make them feel the cold water.**

**Why This Matters:** Readers have built-in defenses against sales pitches, but they have no immunity against feeling understood. Pain-based copy cuts through scepticism because it validates the reader's reality before asking for anything.

---

### 2. Write to One Person in a Bar

**The Scenario:** Maslen's litmus test for tone: imagine your ideal customer sitting across from you at a pub. Would you say to them, "Our synergistic platform leverages cutting-edge AI to optimise your workflow"? Or would you say, "You know that hour you waste every morning sorting emails? This sorts them for you"? The second version wins every time because it sounds like a human talking to a human.

**The Pattern:** Corporate voice creates distance; conversational voice creates connection. **Replace "we are pleased to announce" with "here's the thing."** Replace "utilise" with "use." Replace demographic abstractions ("our target segment") with a single, specific person you can picture.

**Why This Matters:** People don't share, trust, or buy from institutions — they do those things with other people. The moment your copy feels like it was written by a committee, the reader's emotional engagement evaporates.

---

### 3. Get Them Nodding

**The Scenario:** Maslen's technique for opening direct mail: start with three questions the reader cannot help but answer "yes" to. "Do you ever feel like you're working harder but getting less done? Wish you had just two extra hours in your day? Tired of productivity advice that sounds good but never sticks?" By the time they reach your offer, they've already agreed with you three times — creating psychological momentum toward a fourth "yes."

**The Pattern:** Agreement is a habit. Every time a reader mentally says "yes" to your copy, they lower their defence threshold. **Structure your opening as a series of undeniable observations about the reader's own experience,** so that by the time you present your solution, it feels like their idea.

**Why This Matters:** The biggest barrier in copywriting is not informing — it's overcoming inertia. A reader who has already agreed with you is psychologically predisposed to continue agreeing, because humans value internal consistency.

---

### 4. Steal the Idea, Not the Execution

**The Scenario:** Maslen tells the story of seeing a brilliant perfume ad: a single image of a woman in a red dress walking through a crowd in black and white. The idea was "stand out." He stole that core concept — not the image, not the layout, not the tagline — and adapted it for a B2B software client: "While your competitors blend into the background, our clients get noticed." Same psychological trigger, completely different execution.

**The Pattern:** Creative originality is overrated; strategic adaptation is underrated. The best copywriters are magpies — they collect the underlying *ideas* that make campaigns work (isolation, contrast, transformation, belonging) and re-skin them for new contexts. **Copy the structure, copy the psychology, never copy the words.**

**Why This Matters:** Waiting for divine inspiration produces inconsistency. Building a swipe file of *concepts* and learning to recognise the psychological machinery beneath great copy turns writing into a craft you can execute on demand.

## The Maslen Formula

After distilling all 100 ideas, a clear equation emerges:

> **Effective Copy = (Specific Pain × Conversational Voice × Sequential Yeses) − Corporate Jargon**

Here's what this looks like in practice: start by naming a pain so specific it stings (not "boost your productivity" but "stop losing two hours a day to emails you'll never read"). Deliver that message in a voice so conversational it sounds like a friend texting. Build agreement step by step — never ask for the sale until they've nodded at least three times. And ruthlessly delete any phrase that sounds like it came from a boardroom.

## Conclusion

Maslen's 100 ideas distill to one discipline: **write for the one person reading alone, not the boardroom that approved the budget.** Start with their pain, speak like a human, earn agreement step by step, and measure everything by action taken. The best copy doesn't feel like copy at all — it feels like someone finally understanding what you've been trying to say.
